# What Is Fun?

> Status: Living design scratchpad  
> Purpose: capture general hypotheses about fun in this game before they become
> formal mechanics, acceptance criteria, or balance targets.

This is a values document, not a feature specification. An entry can guide a
design experiment without being treated as proof that the mechanic is fun.

## Starting principles

- **Fun is the underdog triumphing over long odds.** The comeback should feel
  earned through a readable choice, not handed out by invisible balancing.
- **Fun is close calls.** Danger should create a meaningful recovery window;
  failure should not feel instantaneous, arbitrary, or already decided.
- **Fun is predictable cause and effect.** The player should be able to connect
  a rule, choice, or ecological change to what happened, even when the outcome
  is uncertain.
- **Fun is planning and execution.** A player should be able to form a plan,
  commit to it, and then watch execution succeed or fail through the interaction
  of cellular rules, terrain, species, and timing.
- **Fun is an extreme low point before an extreme high point.** Darth Vader has
  Luke Skywalker locked in his sights; it feels hopeless. Then Han shows up:
  hooray! The reversal is exciting because the danger was real and the release
  is earned by a timely change.
- **Fun is “Ma.”** Leave meaningful space before the storm: a calm beat, a pause,
  or a quiet pattern that lets anticipation build before the next ecological
  shift, crisis, or opportunity.

## What these principles suggest for this game

These are implications to test, not locked requirements:

- The arms race should create recoverable pressure rather than an invisible
  rubber-band correction.
- Near-misses and reversals need enough time and feedback for the player to
  recognize what saved or endangered the population.
- Simulation uncertainty can remain, but the rules and relevant state need to
  be inspectable in the run history and result summary.
- Upgrade choices should matter before the crisis, while execution should still
  depend on geography, timing, resource access, and species interactions.
- Pressure should sometimes reach a visibly desperate state before the player
  receives or discovers the reversal. Do not erase the low point just to keep
  the simulation comfortable.
- Quiet periods should have purpose. A calm interval can communicate the current
  state, create anticipation, and give the player time to commit to a plan
  before the next pressure wave.

## Risk/reward round cadence

Potential loop to explore: between each simulation round, the player chooses
both an upgrade and an event for the next round. The event offer could include
one safer/favorable option and one dangerous/adverse option:

- the safer option has a positive or neutral ecological effect, but reduces or
  leaves rewards unchanged;
- the riskier option increases the eventual reward while making the following
  simulation more difficult, volatile, or costly.

This gives the player an explicit answer to “Do I want to push for more?” It
also gives the calm between rounds a purpose: inspect the current state, make a
plan, and decide how much danger to accept before execution begins.

This remains a design hypothesis. The event's ecological effect, reward
modifier, visibility, and interaction with the chosen upgrade need to be tested
together. The choice should never be a hidden difficulty multiplier; the player
should understand what is being risked and what the additional reward could be.

## Future prompts

- What does an earned comeback look like in one Forest Edge run?
- Which close calls are exciting, and which only feel like noise or delay?
- Can a player explain why a plan worked without needing to inspect raw logs?
- How much uncertainty makes planning interesting without making cause and
  effect feel arbitrary?
- Which failures make the player want to try a different plan immediately?
- Does choosing a riskier event feel like a deliberate push-your-luck decision
  rather than an obviously correct way to maximize rewards?
- Should the safer and riskier events alter ecology, rules, starting conditions,
  or only the reward calculation?
- How much information should the player receive about an event before choosing
  it?
